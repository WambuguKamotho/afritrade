AfCFTA at Five: What the Tariff Schedule Means for African Commodity Traders

Five Years, Fifty-Four Nations, and a Patchwork of Commitments

The African Continental Free Trade Area entered into force in May 2019, and the first phase of tariff liberalisation began in January 2021. By 2026, most state parties have submitted and begun phasing in their schedules of tariff concessions — but the picture is uneven, and commodity traders who assume a seamless pan-African free trade zone are operating on a misunderstanding of the architecture.

The headline figure — 90% of tariff lines to be liberalised over ten years — obscures a material complexity: the remaining 10% sensitive products list and 3% exclusion list cover precisely the categories most relevant to African commodity trade, including crude oil, processed agricultural products, and certain mineral concentrates.

Rules of Origin: The Operational Chokepoint

For traders, the Rules of Origin (RoO) framework is the day-to-day implementation reality. AfCFTA's RoO require that goods either be wholly obtained in a state party or have undergone substantial transformation — typically defined as a change in tariff heading, a specified value-added threshold, or a combination of both.

For mineral exporters, this creates an immediate challenge. Copper cathode originating in the DRC and transiting through Tanzania qualifies for AfCFTA preferences when exported to South Africa — provided the exporter can demonstrate compliance with the Annex 2 product-specific rules and present a valid Certificate of Origin from the relevant national body.

In practice, most EXIM operators at Wambugu Kamotho report that Certificates of Origin processing adds two to five working days to documentation timelines, and that verification capacity at border posts remains inconsistent.

The Sensitive Products Backstop

Nigeria, Egypt, South Africa, and Ethiopia have placed the largest sensitive product lists, covering items where domestic industries sought protection from intra-African competition. For traders engaged in:

  • Refined petroleum products: Several West African markets maintain protection on finished fuels
  • Fertilizers and agrochemicals: Morocco and Egypt — Africa's two largest phosphate producers — have been careful about liberalisation timelines
  • Processed foods and beverages: Sugar, vegetable oils, and flour-based products frequently appear on sensitive lists

Traders should obtain the current published tariff schedule for each target market directly from the AfCFTA Secretariat portal or the respective national revenue authority before structuring a transaction.

Phase II Negotiations: Services and Investment

With goods tariff liberalisation still rolling out, AfCFTA Phase II negotiations on services, investment, competition policy, and intellectual property are advancing in parallel. For trade finance practitioners, the investment protocol — currently being finalised — has direct implications for the security of cross-border structured commodity finance arrangements.

The protocol is expected to provide a continental framework for investor-state dispute settlement, replacing the current patchwork of bilateral investment treaties. Whether this will be enforceable in practice depends on the willingness of high-FDI states to submit to supranational arbitration.

Practical Implications for Wambugu Kamotho Clients

For any trader structuring intra-African transactions in 2026, three actions are essential: verify that the destination country has deposited its instrument of ratification; obtain the product-specific Rule of Origin for the relevant HS code; and engage an accredited issuing authority for the AfCFTA Certificate of Origin early in the documentation process. Assumptions about seamless tariff-free access remain exactly that — assumptions.
