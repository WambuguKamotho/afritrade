East Africa's Fertilizer Import Deficit: Logistics, Pricing, and the Last-Mile Gap

The Structural Import Dependency

Sub-Saharan Africa applies roughly 17 kg of fertilizer per hectare of arable land, against a global average of 135 kg/ha and an Asian average of over 200 kg/ha. The gap is partly agronomic, partly economic — but it is principally logistical and structural. Africa imports more than 90% of its fertilizer requirements, and in East Africa, the principal import corridor flows through the Port of Mombasa.

Mombasa handles the bulk of Kenya's, Uganda's, Rwanda's, and Eastern DRC's fertilizer imports. In a typical import season (ahead of the long rains, March–May), the port processes volumes of urea, DAP (diammonium phosphate), CAN (calcium ammonium nitrate), and NPK blends from the Black Sea, the Middle East, and increasingly from Morocco (OCP Group's Granuphos and other products).

The Landed Cost Stack

Understanding why fertilizer is expensive at the farmgate in East Africa requires disaggregating the cost stack. A 50 kg bag of DAP priced at USD 580/MT FOB Morocco will typically arrive at the Kenyan farmer at the equivalent of USD 950–1,050/MT, representing a 64–81% markup over the export price. The components of that markup are:

  • Ocean freight (Morocco/Black Sea to Mombasa): USD 35–55/MT, highly volatile and correlated with global bulk shipping rates
  • Port charges, storage, and clearance at Mombasa: USD 40–65/MT, including KEPHIS inspection and KRA customs assessment
  • Inland transport (Mombasa to upcountry depot): USD 80–130/MT for destinations in the Rift Valley; higher for Western Kenya or Uganda
  • Trader margin and local distribution: USD 60–100/MT across multiple intermediary layers
  • Last-mile from agro-dealer to farm: USD 20–40/MT in accessible areas; materially higher in remote zones

The inland transport component — often underweighted in import calculations — is frequently the largest single cost driver for landlocked destinations. A consignment moving from Mombasa to Kampala via the Northern Corridor covers approximately 1,700 km; the equivalent per-MT road freight cost often exceeds the ocean freight from Europe.

The Subsidy Distortion Problem

Government fertilizer subsidies in East Africa — Kenya's NCPB procurement, Tanzania's national subsidy programme, Uganda's periodic support schemes — have historically distorted private market development. Subsidised fertilizer, when it arrives late (as it typically does, given procurement delays), displaces commercial supply and leaves importers holding inventory at the start of the planting season.

The 2022–2023 global fertilizer price spike, driven by the Russia-Ukraine conflict's impact on urea, AN, and potash supply, exposed the vulnerability of subsidy-dependent systems: governments that had not forward-contracted were unable to deliver in time for planting, and farmers who had not established commercial supply relationships had no fallback.

The Strategic Opportunity: Aggregation and Pre-Positioning

For structured fertilizer importers, the East African market presents a clear opportunity in supply aggregation and pre-positioning. Farmers and agro-dealers who can access committed volumes at transparent pre-season prices — rather than navigating the spot market in March — are willing to pay a modest premium for certainty of supply. The model requires:

  • Forward purchase agreements with Black Sea or North African producers, placed in Q4 of the preceding year
  • Vessel charter or booking well ahead of the peak season congestion window at Mombasa (January–February)
  • Bonded warehousing in Nairobi or at upcountry ICD locations (Eldoret, Kisumu) to decouple arrival from distribution
  • Formal partnerships with agro-dealer networks for structured last-mile offtake

Wambugu Kamotho's fertilizer import advisory work has consistently found that the greatest margin compression in the system occurs at the bonded warehouse and inland transport stage — and that these are also the stages where a well-capitalized importer with established logistics relationships can extract the most durable competitive advantage.
