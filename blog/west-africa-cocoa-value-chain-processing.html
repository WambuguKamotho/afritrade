West Africa's Cocoa Value Chain: Why Bean Exports Are a Policy Choice, Not a Constraint

The Bean Export Paradox

Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana together produce between 60% and 65% of the world's cocoa beans in a typical year. Both countries have, for decades, exported the majority of their production as unprocessed dry beans — leaving the transformation into cocoa liquor, butter, powder, and ultimately chocolate to European and North American processors and confectioners.

The per-tonne economics of this arrangement are stark. A metric tonne of Ivorian cocoa beans traded at around USD 9,500 on ICE Futures in early 2026. The same tonne, if processed to cocoa butter and powder, yields products with a combined value of approximately USD 14,000–16,000. Finished dark chocolate — if the value chain extended all the way — would represent a multiple of ten or more.

The question is not whether Africa should process more cocoa. The question is what has prevented it, and what has changed.

The Historical Structural Barriers

The persistence of raw bean exports from West Africa reflects a combination of historical policy choices, trade architecture, and market access constraints:

  • EU tariff escalation: Until recent reforms, the EU applied zero tariff to raw cocoa beans but 7.7–15% on cocoa powder and butter — a classic tariff escalation structure that penalises upstream processing in producing countries
  • Grinding infrastructure deficit: Industrial grinding requires significant capital investment and stable electricity supply — a challenge for markets where power reliability has historically been inconsistent
  • Access to pre-export finance: Processors require working capital to buy beans, process them, and then sell the derivatives — a credit gap that has limited the growth of independent Ivorian and Ghanaian processors
  • Brand and channel control: The major confectionery brands (Mondelez, Nestlé, Mars, Lindt) have historically controlled the branded end of the value chain, making the economics of African-origin finished chocolate difficult in European retail

What Is Changing

Several forces are converging in 2025–2026 to accelerate the structural shift toward in-country processing:

The Ivorian and Ghanaian government floors: The Cocoa Initiative between Accra and Abidjan established a Living Income Differential (LID) of USD 400/MT — a price floor mechanism applied to beans. While the LID has faced implementation challenges (buyers shifted sourcing mix in response), it represented a political commitment to extracting more value from the crop.

EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR): From December 2025, EU importers of cocoa (and seven other commodities) must demonstrate that their supply chains are not linked to deforestation after 31 December 2020. This compliance burden has increased the relative cost advantage of large, traceable, well-documented supply chains — which happen to be the same infrastructure that supports in-country processing.

Growing Asian demand for semi-processed products: China, Indonesia, and Vietnam have become major markets for cocoa butter and powder, and their procurement is less brand-tied than European confectionery. This opens alternative export channels for Ivorian and Ghanaian processors that circumvent European tariff structures.

The Grinding Capacity Picture

Côte d'Ivoire has actively targeted 50% local processing of cocoa production — a target it is approaching, with installed grinding capacity now estimated at 700,000–750,000 MT/year against total production of 2.2–2.4 million MT in recent seasons. Barry Callebaut, Cargill, and OLAM all operate substantial grinding facilities in Abidjan's industrial zones.

Ghana's COCOBOD has been slower to reach equivalent capacity, with local grinding at approximately 35% of production. The development of purpose-built processing zones at the Tema Industrial Area is intended to close that gap by 2028.

Trade Intelligence for Cocoa Mandates

For traders and mandate holders working the West African cocoa sector, 2026 presents a more complex market than the straightforward bean-export model of the previous decade. EUDR compliance documentation is now a deal-qualifier, not an optional add-on. Buyers are asking for plot-level GPS coordinates, deforestation-free certification, and audit trails back to the farm. Exporters who cannot provide this — regardless of bean quality — are experiencing systematic buyer reluctance.

The actionable implication: supply chain documentation, cooperative certification, and deforestation monitoring platforms are no longer sustainability annexes to a trading mandate. They are the core commercial infrastructure.
